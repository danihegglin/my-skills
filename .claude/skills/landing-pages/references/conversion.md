# Conversion: structure, sections and CTAs

A landing page is an argument. The visitor arrives with a question ("is this for me, and is it worth my time?") and a stack of objections. Each section answers the next objection in the order it comes up, then asks for the action.

## Awareness decides length

Eugene Schwartz's awareness levels are still the most useful way to decide how much the page has to do.

| Visitor | Example traffic | What the page must do | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Most aware**: knows you, wants a deal or a reason | Retargeting, email to users, branded search | Offer + CTA. Get out of the way. | Short |
| **Product aware**: knows you, not convinced | Comparison search, review sites | Differentiation, proof, objections | Medium |
| **Solution aware**: knows tools like yours exist | "best X for Y" search, category ads | Why yours, for them, specifically | Medium-long |
| **Problem aware**: feels the pain, doesn't know the fix | Problem-keyword search, content | Name the pain, show the new way, then you | Long |
| **Unaware** | Cold social | Story or hook first; usually a bad fit for a landing page | Long |

The colder the visitor, the more the page explains before asking. The warmer, the sooner the CTA and the less in its way.

## The first screen

At 390px and at 1440px, without scrolling, a visitor should be able to answer:

1. **What is this?** (category in plain words)
2. **Is it for me?** (audience or situation)
3. **Why is it better?** (the outcome or the difference)
4. **What do I do next?** (one primary CTA, plus a lighter secondary if needed)
5. **Should I trust it?** (a hint of proof: a number, logos, a rating, a recognisable name)

And it should *show* the product: a real UI shot, a short loop, an interactive demo, the physical product. A visitor who sees the thing believes the thing.

If the hero can't do all five, the problem is usually the copy, not the layout.

## Default section order

Start here, then cut or reorder for the brief. Not every page needs every section.

1. **Header**: logo, at most 3-4 links (or none), one CTA button
2. **Hero**: headline, subhead, primary CTA, risk reducer ("Free for 14 days, no card"), product visual
3. **Proof strip**: customer logos, a rating, a usage number, or a short quote
4. **Problem / stakes** *(problem-aware traffic)*: the cost of the current way, in the reader's words
5. **How it works**: 3 steps, each concrete, showing how little effort it takes
6. **Benefits**: 3-6 outcomes, each backed by the feature that makes it true and a visual
7. **Deep proof**: testimonials with names, roles, faces, numbers; or one short case study
8. **Comparison** *(solution- and product-aware traffic)*: honest vs. the alternative they're considering
9. **Pricing** *(self-serve)*: or a clear "what it costs" line for sales-led
10. **FAQ**: the real objections: price, switching cost, security, time to value, cancelation
11. **Final CTA**: restate the promise in one line, same CTA, same risk reducer
12. **Footer**: contact, legal (privacy, terms, imprint where required), secondary links

## Section-by-section notes

**Hero.** The headline carries the value; the subhead carries the mechanism ("how") and the audience. Put the CTA within easy reach of both. The visual should be the product doing the thing promised in the headline, cropped to the interesting part, not the whole dashboard at 30% scale.

**Proof strip.** Logos only help if they mean something to this reader. Five recognisable logos beat twenty unknown ones. Greyscale them so they don't fight the hero. If you have no logos, use a number ("2,400 shops"), a rating ("4.8 on Shopify, 310 reviews") or one sharp quote.

**How it works.** Three steps is the convention because it reads as "easy". Each step: a short verb-led title, one sentence, and ideally a small visual. If the real setup has eight steps, show the three that matter and say how long the whole thing takes.

**Benefits.** Lead with the outcome, support with the feature. "Know which customers are about to churn" (benefit) → "Health scores from billing and usage data" (feature). Vary the layouts: a big feature with a large visual, then two smaller ones, then a list. Six identical cards in a grid read as filler.

**Testimonials.** Pick quotes that answer an objection or state a result. Full name, role, company, photo. Edit for length, never for meaning. Put the best one near the CTA it supports.

**Comparison.** Be fair or don't do it. A table that shows competitors failing at everything isn't believed. Compare against the realistic alternative, often "spreadsheets" or "hiring someone".

**FAQ.** Source questions from sales calls and support, not imagination. Answer in 1-3 sentences. Use the FAQ to remove the final objections, not to repeat features.

**Final CTA.** A focused band with the headline promise restated (new words, same idea), the same CTA label as the hero, the same risk reducer.

## CTAs

- **One primary action**, repeated: hero, after the main proof, final band, header. Same wording every time.
- **Label = what they get**, starting with a verb: "Start free trial", "Get the template", "Book a 20-minute demo", "See pricing". Avoid "Submit", "Learn more", "Get started" when something more specific fits.
- **First person sometimes wins** ("Start my trial") and is worth testing, not assuming.
- **Risk reducer right under or beside the button**: "Free for 14 days. No card." "Cancel anytime." "Takes 2 minutes." This line often does more than the button text.
- **Secondary CTA** ("Watch the 2-min demo", "Talk to sales") is visually quieter: outline or text link.
- **Contrast**: the primary button is the most visually prominent interactive element on every screen it appears on. Nothing else uses its colour as a fill.
- **Size**: at least 44px tall on touch, full-width on phones in the hero is fine.

## Social proof

Ranked roughly by persuasive weight:

1. Specific result from a named customer ("Cut churn from 7% to 4.1% in a quarter", Ana Ruiz, Head of Growth, Flock)
2. Usage numbers that are large and exact ("31,482 teams")
3. Recognisable logos relevant to this reader
4. Third-party ratings with count (G2, Capterra, App Store, Trustpilot)
5. Press mentions (only recognisable outlets)
6. Generic praise quotes ("Love it!")

Rules: real names, real photos, real numbers, with permission. Date-sensitive numbers ("10k users") must be true on the day of launch. Put proof next to the claim it proves, not in a separate ghetto.

If proof is thin (new product): show the founders, show the product in detail, offer a strong guarantee or free tier, use a waitlist count, quote beta users by first name and role. Mark gaps as `[NEED: ...]`.

## Pricing on the page

- Show it if self-serve. Hidden pricing reads as "expensive" and pushes visitors to leave and search.
- 2-3 tiers. Highlight one. Name tiers by who they're for, not gems or metals.
- Monthly/annual toggle shows savings in money, not just percent.
- Every tier has a CTA. The highlighted tier's CTA matches the page's primary CTA.
- Put an FAQ about billing (trial, cancelation, refunds, seats) right below.

## Forms and friction

- Ask for the minimum: email is often enough. Every extra field costs conversions; justify each one.
- Labels visible (not placeholder-only), correct `type`, `autocomplete`, and `inputmode`.
- Inline validation on blur, clear error text, never clear the form on error.
- The button says what happens ("Join the waitlist", "Send me the guide").
- After submitting: a real confirmation state that says what happens next and when.
- SSO buttons ("Continue with Google") can beat email forms for B2B SaaS.

## Navigation and focus

A landing page for paid traffic often has no nav at all: logo + CTA. A homepage that also serves as a landing page can keep 3-4 links. Never put a link in the hero that competes with the CTA. Footer links are fine; nobody leaves from the footer before deciding.

## Mobile

- Design the 390px layout first. It's most of the traffic and the hardest constraint.
- Headline wraps to 2-4 lines max; adjust the clamp, not the copy, unless the copy is too long.
- The product visual must stay legible: crop tighter on mobile or use a different, simpler visual.
- Tap targets ≥ 44px, ≥ 8px apart. Primary CTA reachable with a thumb.
- A sticky bottom CTA bar can help on long pages. Show it after the hero scrolls out, hide it at the final CTA.
- No hover-only information.

## Message match

The page must continue the sentence the visitor read before clicking. If the ad says "Gift subscriptions for Shopify", the hero headline should say gift subscriptions and Shopify. Same promise, same words, same offer. For multiple traffic sources, make variants of the hero (Astro makes per-route variants cheap) rather than one vague page.

## Measuring and testing

- Track: primary CTA clicks (per position), form starts, form completes, scroll depth to key sections. A `data-cta="hero"` attribute plus a privacy-friendly analytics tool (Plausible, Fathom, Umami, PostHog) is enough.
- Test big things first: headline/offer, hero visual, CTA offer (trial vs. demo), page length, presence of pricing. Button colour tests are a waste of traffic.
- Need roughly a few hundred conversions per variant before trusting a result. Low-traffic pages should rely on qualitative signals instead: 5-second tests, session recordings, asking five target users what the page offers.
